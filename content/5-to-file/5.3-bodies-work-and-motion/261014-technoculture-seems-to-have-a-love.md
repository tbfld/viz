### Existentialism, quantified


![[17993671782029234-1.jpg]]


This image is utterly generic, which is why — or , better, *how* — it’s interesting.

Technoculture seems to have a love affair with this kind of “proxemics” theory, first articulated (AFAIK) by the cultural anthropologist Edward T. Hall in the early 1960s — I’d argue as part of a quickly growing attention to the spatial aspects of sociability and lifeworlds (for ex, Edmund “Ted” Carpenter’s 1959 quasi-monograph *Eskimo*, which experimented with book design to convey unfamiliar spatial constructs. That awareness a basic tool for anthropologists, but it wasn’t until the early ’90s (read: the internet) that proxemics took off, often under names like “zones of engagement” — and, strangely, mostly by people working <cough> “at the intersection of” quant marketing, business school, and social psychology. 

The internet is awash in images on this subject, so it’s easy to overlook basic features of specific examples — the kinds of features that would be bog-standard in an traditional art-historical analysis. The most/least obvious is the *isolation* of the (invariably *masculine*) figure*, whose features are presented in the anatomical idiom typical of 3D-rendered figures): other people, from intimate to public, have been replaced with a metric diagram. Images like this often look down on the figure (perhaps because of the affinity of this thinking with architectural optimization), but the viewpoint of this image is eye-level. We could also look at the use of color, the differently shaped ‘fields,’ the changes in line quality, and so on — but traditional art-historical analysis attend to fine details on the assumption that they were deliberate. In CGI images (i.e., in a skyrocketing percentage of the images we see), intentions are so mediated by default settings, menus, options, and automation that we need to fundamentally rethink the relations between intent and artifact.

Taken together, these features (the ways the human form is conceived, the ways others are represented, the ways perspectives and measures are integrated) make this image not just *about* sociability but also an image *of* sociability — and a fairly forlorn one at that. In poetic terms, we could see him as the individual at the heart (or lack thereof) of the “lonely crowd,” except that that cultural reference — to David Riesman, Nathan Glazer, and Reuel Denney’s [book of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lonely_Crowd) — is the better part of a century old. Even those authors, writing at a time when McCarthyism was consuming the US, understood that what they described was the product of western history and of capitalism in particular. So, while we could quibble about the exact mechanics of *how* images of this kind are political, there’s no doubt that they are.

Similarly, the object qualities of the image (how it was generated, how the ‘creator’ related to *his* tools, how it’s an arbitrary example of a type of illustration that’s widely and freely available, how it serves as a basic reference in fields like marketing) add an additional political dimension, but a politics of a different kind — one in which, *as this image demonstrates*, sociability has been reduced to a byproduct of classifications and measurements rather than meaningful social relations. In this view (which some would call *neoliberal*), “intimate” isn’t necessarily someone with whom you share secrets, it’s someone who *gets in your space.* To understand this kind of “politics” requires a different kind of theory — the kind laid out in, for example, Steffen Mau’s *[The Metric Society]([https://isbn.nu/978-1-509-53040-3](https://isbn.nu/9781509530410))* (2019) and 
Justin Joque’s *[Revolutionary Mathematics](https://isbn.nu/9781788734004)* (2022).
