*Note: This essay has been sitting on my hard drive for a decade, and I’m delighted to finally get it off. I’ve update a few things and tweaked a few others, but it remains substantially the same. — Ted*

 
For decades, Edward Tufte was — and for many still is — the most widely known name in the field of visualization, and with good reason. His visual acuity is immense and intense, and he’s dedicated much of his life to encouraging others to cultivate this critical awareness — in particular, by attuning them to the importance of attentive and accurate representations of information and data. He’s worked tirelessly to show how lazy, thoughtless, or pandering charts and graphs can have serious consequences, ranging from pervasive mediocrity to sudden catastrophe. More than that, he’s a vigorously entrepreneurial one-man band, an impresario and pamphleteer who researches, curates, writes, publishes, promotes, and distributes his ideas and books via his website and in person. Starting in the mid-1980s he has criss-crossed the US, promoting his vision of a virtuous vision in day-long lectures held in corporate meeting rooms packed with people who pay \$300–400 to learn from him and get a boxed set of his books. These efforts have succeeded wildly: as of 2007 one source claimed there were *1.4 million* copies of his titles in print; by 2009 it was said to be 1.5 million copies,[^1] and by 2011 it was almost 2 million.[^2] Even if those figures were exaggerated tenfold or even a hundredfold, the actual numbers would still be blockbusters for nonfiction.

For many years journalists vied to outdo each other with celebratory stories about Tufte, who was invariably introduced with the definite article *the*. He was “the man who makes sense of numbers,”[^3] “the master of graphics and design,”[^4] “the information sage,”[^5] “the data artist,”[^6] and “the minister of information.”[^7] More than that, he was “the Galileo of graphics”[^8] and “the da Vinci of data.”[^9] But even mere Renaissance polymathery wasn’t enough for James Surowiecki, who named his 1999 article about Tufte’s apparition at the Bose Corporation “Sermon on the Mountain.”[^10] In it, Tufte’s books were “bibles of design,” and “\[w\]hile his arrival was not quite equivalent to Moses coming down from Mount Sinai, it was close enough.”

With hype like that, it’s small wonder that for decades Tufte’s name was brandished as a banner by countless office workers aiming to differentiate themselves or even to make a difference. Facing a tough job-interview question about effective communications? EDWARD TUFTE. Looking to impress a client? EDWARD TUFTE. Facing yet another pitch, presentation, interface, or website design? EDWARD TUFTE. Need a book or four facing out on the shelf to signify that you ‘get it’? EDWARD TUFTE. Or — in my case — writing a cultural history of data visualization? *You mean like EDWARD TUFTE?* (Not exactly, no…) *Have you ever heard of EDWARD TUFTE?* (Yes…) *I* ***love*** *EDWARD TUFTE.* (Well…)

No discussion of the recent history of visualization would be complete without acknowledging him — if not as an authoritative arbiter then at least as a pop phenomenon. And yet, somehow, historians and theorists of visualization avoided him like he’s the devil. Johanna Drucker’s *Graphesis* acknowledged his existence in a single footnote. Orit Halpern’s *Beautiful Data* discreetly namechecked his 2006 book *Beautiful Evidence* alongside another obscure 2009 (also named *Beautiful Data*) — again, in a footnote.[^11] In the 2012 800-page doorstop *Handbook of Visual Culture*, he’s mentioned just once, at the end, in the exhausting 100-page *bibliography*, filed under — take a deep breath — “The Sociology and Anthropology of Visual Art, Techniques, Media and Communications (Including the Use of Visual Technologies Such as Photography and Film in Anthropological Research),” and within that, under the subheading “Recent Research in Institutional Visualization (Statistics, Medicine, Social Embodiments, Scientific Visualization, Bio-medical Technologies, etc).[^12]” And though his works are noted in a dozen bibliographies scattered throughout Springer’s titanic 950-page *Handbook of Data Visualization*, he’s rarely mentioned in the text — and when he is, it’s to credit him as the source of an image.[^13] More recent works like Miles Kimball and Charles Kostelnick’s *Visible Numbers* (2016)[^14] or Michael Friendly and Howard Wainer’s *History of Data Visualization and Graphic Communication* (2021)[^15] ritually invoke him more often, but most mentions boil down to tacit acknowledgments that he’s famous for being famous. The only passage that critically considers Tufte in either of those books quietly damns him with ambivalence: his “progressive, teleological story” invites a “Whiggish history” that we need to “avoid” by paying careful attention to everything he ignored — an amazing thing to say about someone who’s often billed as a historian. In short, in humanistic discussions of data visualization Tufte is almost *persona non grata*.

The feelings are mutual. When it comes to citing others’ scholarly work in the field, the common coin in scholarship, Tufte has been at best grudging. In his first book about visualization, ***The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*** (1983), he nimbly avoided acknowledging nearly a century of plain-as-day precedents. In one footnote he allowed that the French scholar Jacques Bertin’s monumental 1967 work *The Semiology of Graphics: Diagrams, Networks, Maps*[^16] was “important,”[^17] but he didn’t say that it was the first systematic survey of information-oriented graphics — a fact that surely would have interested some readers. He mentioned Walter Herdeg’s 1974 *Graphis/Diagrams*[^18] in passing, blandly noting that it’s “described by its publisher \[as\] ‘An international review demonstrating convincingly that statistical and diagrammatic graphics do not have to be dull.’” The book’s subtitle was ***The Graphic Visualization of Abstract Data***, and its publisher, Graphis International — which played an immense role in fostering a global culture of graphic design since WW2 — had issued four completely different editions of it in the decade before Tufte put his own book. Tufte’s faint praise for the first edition, a “fine book on graphics,” would hardly lead readers to envision what it was: a compendium published at the height of ’70s pop graphics overflowing with hundreds of fascinating experiments in imagining what data might looks like. Of Willard C. Brinton’s seminal 1914 ***Graphic Methods for Presenting Facts***[^19] (1914), Tufte blandly says the 400-page book, which overflows with clippings that Brinton had collected from a boggling range of publications, was just one of ten “textbooks and handbooks of statistical graphics \[and\] manuals for computer graphics programs” published between 1914 and 1981. Brinton, an engineer and inventor, had worked tirelessly with professional societies to promote the “graphic presentation of facts” as the key to modernizing industrial society — work that likely contributed to the other nine books Tufte doesn’t name. It might not be surprising that Tufte didn’t mention Charles Baker and Walter Grether’s 1954 military-oriented monograph ***Visual Presentation of Information***[^20] because it was officially declassified in 1979 — though the godfather of industrial designer, [Henry Dreyfuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Dreyfuss), had drawn heavily on it in his seminal 1960 book *The Measure of Man*).[^21] But it *is* surprising that Tufte — who was trained in political science and taught statistics at Princeton in 1975 — didn’t mention that the American Statistical Association’s 1976 meeting resulted in a book called ***The Graphic Presentation of Statistical Information***.[^22] And, while we can’t be sure, he may well have know while he was writing his first book that another with a similar name was in the works: ***Graphical Methods for Data Analysis***, whose authors included William Cleveland, a prominent authority in the field, and Paul Tukey,[^23] son of the famed statistician John Tukey, who Tufte claimed as the inspiration for his first book. It’s not just that there happen to be a few scattered precedents and parallels for Tufte’s book. These many permutations on a handful of words has already created a crowded field — and Tufte’s own title, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, was probably the result of a process of elimination.

As the Captain puts it in the 1967 Paul Newman film *Cool Hand Luke*, [what we've got here is failure to communicate](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=452XjnaHr1A). On the one hand, humanistic scholars barely acknowledge Tufte at all, let alone explain *why* they ignore his work. On the other, and from the very beginning, Tufte has avoided clearly acknowledging seventy years of precedents, let alone current scholarly debates. In part, this disconnect probably stems from their differing aims. Tufte’s books are mostly concerned with practice: they aim to promote, through example and explanation, what he calls “graphical excellence.” Scholars may agree with those ideals, but overall their work is more concerned with interpreting the past than producing the future. Even so, it’s hard not to suspect there might be other reasons that so many scholars managed not to talk about him for the last forty-odd years.

In many ways Tufte is a non-scholar’s idea of a scholar: opinionated, obsessive, lecturing, hectoring, a man of big ideas. And his books — with their spacious layout, ostentatiously elegant typography, dazzlingly eclectic images, and fastidious-looking footnotes — look ‘scholarly.’ Tufte’s Wikipedia entry, which like many celebrity entries is a meticulously maintained and unverifiable exercise in hagiography, played up this relentlessly restless life of the mind for many years:

> He is intensely critical in the self-editing process. He pulls in and casts out ideas from books, journals, posters, auction catalogs, and other less common sources. He invites others to critique his work in-progress and may nurture dozens of ideas over months in various states of growth and fruition. He deletes almost every photograph he takes. Over time, he deletes most of what he writes on his own forum, ET Notebooks. Every printing of every book corrects numerous small blemishes, ranging from color registration to kerning and hinting.[^24]

Despite his formidable erudition and vivid insights, though, his books are anything but comprehensive or fair-minded. On the contrary, they’re lavishly designed rants bristling with moralizing and bombast. People he approves of are “pioneers” and “inventors” and their work radiates “excellence” and “integrity,” whereas those he dislikes are heaped with abuse. In one discussion alone, just a few pages long, he describes his targets as “scandalous,” “stupefying,” “hapless,” and “ludicrous and corrupt.”[^25] When he plumbs the abysmal depths of this corruption, he overshoots the bounds of criticism by a few centuries and at times ends up sounding like a preacher fulminating against the prince of lies:

> Lying graphics cheapen the graphical art everywhere. Since the lies often show up in news reports millions of images are printed. When a chart on television lies, it lies tens of millions of times over; when the *New York Times* chart lies, it lies 900,000 times over to a great many important and influential readers.[^26]

Tufte’s critics, who for decades were either very few or very quiet (see the image below), have pointed at this tendency as a weakness in his work, when — I suspect — it was its *strength*. After all, the same ideas presented in moderate and practical language would have met the same fate as countless other ‘best practices.’ But in Tufte’s hands they transformed the obscure business of designing charts and graphs into an ideological arsenal — and transformed him from a self-published author into a world-renowned public intellectual of design. But his talent, ambition, or ambition didn’t exist in a vacuum: they flourished, above all, in the early years of the internet. His message, with its double vision — of *quantitative data*, on the one hand, and *visual presentation*, on the other — was ideally suited to a world increasingly smitten with commanding quantitative overviews rather than squishy realities. He pushed that message 24/7 with entrepreneurial zeal, and his promotional operations — a discussion-driven website that promoted his seminars that promoted his books that promoted his website — set a DIY standard for online/offline marketing integration. And he delivered this all with great gusto, sputtering with flaming and bristling with fisking (“point-by-point debunking”[^27]). For a rough-and-tumble online world that was lionizing provocateurs like Ayn Rand and Richard Dawkins, Tufte’s ‘brand’ was brilliant. The net result: he made the bloodless business of statistical graphics into a bloodsport.

It’s impossible to write about Tufte’s ideas without addressing Tufte himself because the overwhelming majority of his writing center not on factual accounts or impersonal methods but, instead, on his *opinions*. When you strip them away, much of what’s left is a *cri de coeur*, so his basic ideas can be boiled down to a few sentences. He believes that visually conveying information is vitally important, so we should approach it in intellectually, aesthetically, and morally rigorous ways. Those imperatives demand that visual presentations be direct, honest, and economical — measured not by the contextual uncertainties of the information itself but by the cognitive certainties of the user. The cartographic historian Denis Wood gave a concise and dispassionate summary of Tufte’s recommendations in a review of *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*:

> Graphical displays should...show the data; avoid distorting what the data have to say; induce the viewer to think about substance rather than methodology, graphic design or the technology of graphic production; present many numbers in a small space; make large data sets coherent; encourage the eye to compare different pieces of data; reveal the data at several levels of detail; serve a reasonably clear purpose; and be closely integrated with their statistical and verbal descriptions.[^28]

In practice, though, the public reception of Tufte’s ideas has often translated into two basic injunctions, one *for* and one *against*. He’s *for* what he calls a high “data–ink ratio”: use as little ink as possible to convey as much information as possible. And he’s *against* what he calls “chartjunk” — basically, *ix-nay* on the *ecoration-day*. His other major contributions are (literally) minor: emoji-esque “spark-lines” (graphs shrunk to the size of a word so they can be inserted into a line of text) and “small multiples” (“postage-stamp size\[d] illustrations…sequenced over time like the frames of a movie”[^29]). Both are victories of branding; neither is especially innovative.

Convictions like Tufte’s often translate into bodies of work that are aggressively minimalist, like the architectural theorist Adolph Loos’s (1870–1933) famous claim that “ornament is a crime.” But Tufte is a voracious aesthete and collector who’s spent decades scouring the world for examples — almost all of them *print* rather than electronic. So, instead, the result has been more like a coffee-table edition of Martin Luther’s 99 Theses: lavishly produced books that argue for the virtues of minimalism. And that tension, between the extravagance and the austere, is only the most obvious of a tangle of contradictions that define Tufte’s work: occasionally erudite yet hopelessly bombastic, dazzlingly eclectic yet bizarrely monomaniacal, and sneeringly elitist yet demagogically populist.

The purest expression of this bundle of contradictions could be found for many years in his Wikipedia entry, under the header “Criticism of Tufte”[^30] (*not*, note, of his *ideas*): he “uses a presentation format to present his opposition to PowerPoint presentations” (*zzzing!*), he “presents his theories to large audiences (400+) in a one-way format without soliciting audience input, while espousing the need to engage audiences,” and “\[h\]is recommendation of tools include \[the software package\] LaTeX, which may still be the academic choice for writing papers with lots of math, but which may not be practical for business users.”[^31] In popular discussions of Tufte’s work, the possibility that he might just be mistaken — say, that it mostly doesn’t matter how we visualize information, that we can imagine it with the freedom of art rather than than in the shackles of commerce, or that we can (or — heaven forfend!— *should*) pimp it out with total abandon — were, for decades, unthinkable. Those possibilities might seem perverse, but at a time when creative experiments with how people imagine information have transformed everything around us, from children’s schoolbooks to architectural façades, imposing austerely functionalist demands on the visual display of quantitative information isn’t just a narrow design question — it’s as political as demanding that all writing be functional because, ultimately, ‘information’ is a form of writing.

Tufte has had a handful of vocal critics, notably a few practicing graphic designers with the stature and the platform to challenge his ideas. In a 2003 essay in the *Design Observer* the designer-writer [Jessica Helfand](https://www.jessicahelfand.com/) described him as “Yale statistician emeritus, publisher, millionaire — and graphic designer,” teeing him up to take a blunt whack at him: his “expertise is not only self-proclaimed — it is also deeply and irrevocably self-serving.”[^32] His theories follow “the principles of Evelyn Wood speed-reading,” his understanding of design “reductivist ad absurdum,” and his focus on cognition “a poorly veiled attempt at reshaping design parlance with himself as its single and uncontested author.”[^33] Helfand’s ire had been piqued in part by an article Tufte had published some weeks earlier in the September issue of *WiReD* magazine, “PowerPoint Is Evil: Power Corrupts. PowerPoint Corrupts Absolutely.”[^34] It introduced one of Tufte’s other enduring legacies, a sneering disdain for Microsoft PowerPoint, which he likened to a drug that “induced stupidity, turned everyone into bores, wasted time, and degraded the quality and credibility of communication.” A few months later, Helfand’s then-husband and a *very* respected designer and writer [William Drenttel](https://a-g-i.org/user/williamdrenttel/) followed up with another broadside that, though more delicately worded, his was brutal in a different way.[^35] “Almost everything” Tufte said about PowerPoint “was penned two years earlier, by [Ian Parker](https://www.newyorker.com/contributors/ian-parker), in an essay in the *New Yorker*” called “Absolute Powerpoint: Can a Software Package Edit Our Thoughts?”[^36] — “an excellent piece of cultural criticism \[that\] was widely discussed and received broad coverage in the media,” and was “all over the web.” Tufte, he argued, was “getting away with \[a\] well-executed publicity campaign” that (not for the first time) exploited the tragedy of a space shuttle blowing up.

The second shuttle disaster had happened several months earlier, on 1 Feb 2003. During launch, chunks of the *Columbia*’s insulating foam had broken off of one wing, and when the shuttle reentered the atmosphere the intense heat disintegrated wing’s internal structure. NASA had known about this risk for years, and in the case of the *Columbia* they consulted with Boeing about it. At the time, Tufte had been working on his fourth book, *Beautiful Evidence* (2006), and he told an interviewer

> I was writing a chapter…on the subject of the sculptural pedestal, which led to my thinking of what's up on the pedestal — the great leader. I did a few pages on a statute of Stalin in Budapest, which was gradually torn down; all that's left is this flat rectangle of ground. I thought, “Great leader up on a pedestal, talking; that’s like PowerPoint.” And then the *Columbia* went down. I looked at the slides Boeing did during the flight, which indicated no danger from foam hitting the wing. NASA believed it, and we’ve seen what happened. Those were in PowerPoint. It’s not that PowerPoint brought the Columbia down, but the method of presentation broke up the argument into tiny fragments, and it’s intensely hierarchical – no sentences, just little phrases.[^37]

The free-associative style of this passage offers a useful insight into Tufte’s approach — in particular, how the wide-ranging works he jumbles together mask a political subtext. But what seemed to offend Drenttel most was how Tufte went on to cast the disaster as a chance to promote his criticism of PowerPoint by rushing out a chapter as a \$7 booklet, “The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint.”[^38] Tufte told the same interviewer, 

> I wanted to get it out fast and have an effect…. We printed 20,000, and we’re going to do a second printing. They've sold mostly through my website. It’s really come alive. It’s the only success story in e-commerce!

Drenttel saw this — rightly, I think — as a crass effort to repeat his success six years earlier, when his analysis of the first shuttle disaster, in 1986 involving *Challenger*, had brought him internet celebrity.

In his 1997 book *Visual Explanations: Images and Quantities, Evidence and Narrative* Tufte had argued that what doomed the Space Shuttle *Challenger* a decade earlier wasn’t be found in the spacecraft’s design, manufacturing, or testing, or even NASA’s management. Instead, the decisive failure took place when the manufacturer of one component, Morton Thiokol, faxed badly designed statistical graphs to support their argument that NASA should postpone the *Challenger*’s launch that day due poor weather. Tufte’s argument was unambiguous: “Had the correct scatterplot or data table been constructed, no one would have dared to risk the *Challenger* in such cold weather.”[^39] And he delivered his conclusion like a drumbeat: “The charts were unconvincing; the argument against the launch failed; the Challenger blew up.”[^40]

As proof, Tufte redesigned the graphs according to his theoretical principles — and with his trademark pseudo-classical typography. Amidst the oceans of ink and rivers of pixels spilled extolling the elegance of his redesign, though, one devilish detail was forgotten: whether his redesigns would have *worked*. In the decade separating the *Challenger* debacle and Tufte’s analysis, the finicky fax machines of the mid-1980s, which relied on thermally printed ‘curly’ paper, had given way to scanners and laser printers. Late-1990s high-res devices might have been able to reproduce the nuances of Tufte’s typography, but the older fax machines were notorious for mangling fine details — especially when documents were fed to the machine at an angle. If Morton Thiokol had used Tufte’s posh design, numbers in his charts would have been mangled, possibly beyond recognition and therefore beyond *cognition*.

Nevertheless, Tufte’s thesis was widely accepted as gospel, and it quickly became one of the emerging digerati’s go-to ‘case studies’ for ’splainin the internet and other new technologies. Alongside Kodak’s failure to recognize the threat of digital photography and the Betamax-vs-VHS videotape ‘format wars,’ Tufte’s focus on the importance of visual design became an all-purpose nostrum for companies struggling to adapt to the internet: his principles applied to everything from web design to the sudden emergence of data analysis as the center of organizational activity.

But the *Challenger* had blown up in 1986, in the early days of Reagan’s lame-duck phase, and Tufte’s book came out over a decade later in 1997, amidst the swelling euphoria of the dotcom bubble and run-up to Clinton’s impeachment. The world wasn’t exactly waiting for a self-appointed visualization aficionado (or anyone else, for that matter) to issue a verdict on it. And with good reason: in 1986 the physicist Richard Feynman had closed the book on the *Challenger* with a legendary demonstration. During a nationally televised hearing, he dunked a sample of the *Challenger*’s failed component, a flexible “O-ring” gasket, in a glass of ice water to show how it lost resiliency at low temperatures — and could cause the kind of failure that brought down the shuttle. At a time when conspiratorial claims that NASA had faked the moon landing were becoming paradigmatic, Feynman’s demonstration had settled the matter with a finality unparalleled in the annals of US catastrophes. So, when Tufte raised the subject a decade later, he was playing the part of the amateur sleuth trying to reopen a case — a grievance-fueled gambit ideally suited to the internet era.
