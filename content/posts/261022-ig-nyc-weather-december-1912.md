This chart of NYC’s weather for December 1912 ran in *Heating and Ventilating Magazine*\[1] some time in 1913–14, then was reprinted in Willard Brinton’s *Graphic Methods for Presenting Facts* (1914, p. 93). Nearly **seventy years later**, Edward Tufte lavished praise on a derivative 1980 NYT weather graphic in his *Visual Display of Quantitative Info* (1983).\[2] The NYT’s version may be more ‘complex’ (a full year, a few bells and whistles), but I think he showcased the later one and ignored the earlier for a different reason: he didn’t want to steer his readers toward Brinton’s work, which revealed how derivative his own book was.

Brinton’s *Graphic Methods* (1914)\[3] and *Graphic Presentation* (1939)\[4] were **the first two books** to present visualization as a distinct field and to systematize it — in a practical, even-handed tone that contrasts with Tufte’s ranty bombast. Yet Tufte mentions Brinton just once, in a table listing several “more recent\[?] publications” guilty of the nemesis he was furiously trying to brand as “chartjunk.” Tufte clearly studied *Graphic Methods*: he counted all 255 graphics to could conclude that a mere 12% (less than all the others) included “moiré vibrations,” i.e., the crosshatching technique derived from drafting. Brinton’s feat was especially impressive because all but two of the other books dated from 1952–1981, when higher-quality papers and color printing — which, as Tufte knew well, helped to obsolesce the crosshatching — had become far more accessible.\[5]

This is just one example of (again, imo) a Tufte’s penchant for obscuring his sources and precedents. There are names for that, and they aren’t very nice.
