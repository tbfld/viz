More: a fine example of transparent spacekitsch.

{{{ In February 1962, Astronaut John H. Glenn Jr. looks into a globe, technically the "Celestial Training Device" at the Aeromedical Laboratory at Cape Canaveral, Florida.

In February 1962, Astronaut John H. Glenn Jr. looks into a globe, technically the “Celestial Training Device” at the Aeromedical Laboratory at Cape Canaveral, Florida. Glenn became the first American to orbit Earth that same month.Image Credit: NASA }}}

But where the rocket-sci guys gathered around the Farquhar / Torica transparent globe tapped into the venerable architect-with-his-model trope, this one contributed to a different one: visual plays on planets and heads. A face on the moon (for example) might seem like a timeless belief, but it wasn't at all — there were a handful of scattered sorta-mentions over the last millennium (e.g., Plutarch’s “On the Face in the Moon’s Orb” \[ca. 100CE]), but it wasn’t until Georges Méliès’s 1902 “A Trip to the Moon” that things took off, so to speak. After that, it became a beloved trope among animators, whose work propagated it with so widely and quickly that it was ~naturalized — something everyone just seems to know somehow — more or less overnight. Images like this NASA PR one are diff, but they respond to one of the same conceptual challenges: how to visually convey the fundamental tension that aviation and spaceflight was introducing, namely, an abrupt transformation in the *scale* of humanity vis-à-vis the earth.
