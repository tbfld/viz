Attack of the Puppet People (1958) 
 
Sally Reynolds (played by June Kenney) is shrunk to 6” high by a troubled dollmaker. Out-of-scale people was a favorite postwar media trope, from children’s lit (The Little Prince \[1943], Stuart Little \[1945], etc), to pulp fiction (e.g., *The Incredible Shrinking Man* \[1957]), to movies (e.g., *Attack of the Fifty Foot Women*, *Fantastic Voyage* \[1966]), and *many* TV series (*Land of the Giants* \[19658 — 1970], *Voyage to the Bottom of the Sea* \[1964 — 1968]) and episodes (*Outer Limits*, *Twilight Zone*, *Lost in Space*, *Star Trek*, to say nothing of cartoons). It was also the central premise of “expanded media” (XXXXXX) — notably, Disneyland’s Monsanto-sponsored Adventures Through Inner Space, which had roots in the theme park’s Alice in Wonderland ride starkly differed from it in its technocratic emphasis. These narratives and ‘experiences‘ often had a pronounced gender dimension — for example, in the 1956 *Shrinking Man* novel on which the film was based, as the main character progressively (or maybe *regressively*) shrinks, he worries he won’t be able to ‘satisfy’ his wife, is harassed by a p_dophile, and so on. In another way, the instability in the size of women placed them — sometimes implicitly, sometimes explicitly — on a qualitative scale ranging from vulnerable plaything (as in this film) to malevolent monster (e.g., fifty-foot women). I’ll leave the question **why** this metric instability became such a popular trope in the postwar period: I’ll leave **how** it came to pass to a later post. (🎩 WY for this image)
